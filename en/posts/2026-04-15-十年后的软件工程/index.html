Software Engineering in Ten Years

April 15, 2026 · 2 min · Honghui Liu | Translations:
  • 中文

Imagining the world ten years from now is an interesting thing. Although most human predictions may be wrong, I still want to give it a try, especially for the software engineering industry that is currently undergoing the most dramatic transformation.

The number of practitioners in the software engineering industry will increase dramatically, and market competition will become incredibly fierce. In the past, this work was seen as a high-barrier technical profession, where only a few practitioners could enjoy the benefits. Now, with the support of large models, the cost of programming has been crushed to almost zero, and anyone can easily become a top programmer through large models. Natural language is the ultimate form of programming language. Then, those who got on board early and enjoyed the benefits due to the times will no longer exist. Once the entry barrier of an industry is low enough, it will bring tremendous competitive pressure, just like stock trading—if you’re not truly passionate, the ultimate result is to be mediocre, with no excess returns, unable to become a top-level figure in this industry.

The form of human-computer interaction will undergo a major change, and the terminal market may see new dark horse companies emerge to revolutionize the current market. This is a naturally deduced result: since the cost of programming approaches zero infinitely, could there be a new form of human-computer interaction, where each user has their own personalized interactive experience? This new form is exciting just to think about.

Besides these two points, I also thought of:

A large amount of work will be replaced by AI automation, the value of repetitive work will become lower and lower, and humans need to spend more energy on creative work.

The interpretability of intelligence emergence is also a very important issue, which will enable subsequent researchers to better train large models. AI researchers will increasingly take on the identity of scientists.
